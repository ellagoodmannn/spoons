spoons

A formal investigation of the humble messengers, spoons.
Welcome to the website that documents the spoons made by Ella Goodman for her degree project.

About

Unlike most designed objects, the spoon hasn’t changed much in the past five thousand years. Complete re-imaginations of spoons are sparse, which I attribute to the spoon’s expertise at serving its intended purpose—a vessel for foods that cannot be eaten by hand. When innovations to spoons are made, they almost always maintain the basic formal elements: the bowl and the handle. In new variations, the element that seems to radicalize the spoon the most is not the shape of the bowl or the spoon-to-handle ratio, but ornamentation and the number of tools available per spoon-unit—take the spork or spife as examples.

This project aims to question the seamless hand-to-mouth experience of the spoon. Why was the spoon standardized as a shallow oval bowl with a flat long handle? How does the experience change without a rigid handle and bowl? Why is this the primary vessel for eating a certain type of food? How could spoons be developed for specific purposes again like during the Paleolithic era? Since the Middle Ages, why have spoons played such a major role in our lives, particularly in our early years as infants, and our later ones as elders?

While many objects could be formally investigated, I found the spoon particularly compelling. It is the most primitive utensil, and its form is composed of two simple shapes: an oval and an elongated rectangle. As nations and tech companies compete to create increasingly advanced artificial intelligence systems, civilization remains deeply content with the humble spoon.
